## What you’re seeing

1. A bank pays **100% interest** a year. Paid once, $1 becomes $2.
2. Split the interest into n smaller payments that each earn interest too, and the <span class="c-cyan">balance</span> climbs in more, smaller jumps.
3. More compounding always helps, but less and less each time. The <span class="c-amber">final amount</span> never passes a ceiling.
4. That ceiling is <span class="c-lime">e = 2.71828…</span>: the most continuous growth can ever give you.

<Equation tex={String.raw`e = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}`} />

## Why e and not 2 or 3?

Every exponential aˣ has a slope that is proportional to its height. Only one base makes that proportion exactly **1**.

<OwnDerivative client:visible />

<Equation tex={String.raw`\frac{d}{dx}\,a^x = (\ln a)\,a^x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dx}\,e^x = e^x`} caption="At a = e, the slope is the height." />

## e meets i

Put an imaginary number in the exponent and growth turns into **rotation**. The rate of change of e^iθ is i · e^iθ: always the same size as the point, but turned a quarter-turn. So the point never moves outward. It travels around the unit circle.

<EulerCircle client:visible />

<Equation tex={String.raw`e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0`} caption="Euler’s identity: five fundamental constants in one line." />

Turn on **(1 + iθ/n)ⁿ** to see this as compound interest again. Each step adds a small sideways nudge, and as n grows the polygon wraps onto the circle exactly.
